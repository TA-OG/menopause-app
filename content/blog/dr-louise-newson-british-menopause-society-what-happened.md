If you have been anywhere near menopause content in the last two years, you have
seen the headline in some form: **Dr Louise Newson, struck off.**

Except that is not what happened. And the gap between what people think happened
and what actually happened matters, because a lot of women are currently making
decisions about their own hormones based on a half-remembered headline.

So here is the boring, checkable version. Dates, sources, no spin.

## What actually happened, in one paragraph

On 30 September 2024, BBC Panorama broadcast "The Menopause Industry Uncovered",
which examined prescribing at Newson Health, the private menopause clinic founded
by Dr Louise Newson. Around the same time, the British Menopause Society removed
Dr Newson from its online menopause specialist register, citing concerns that
aspects of her practice did not accord with established guidance. The Care Quality
Commission said it was looking into information of concern. Dr Newson rejected the
criticism and defended her prescribing as individualised and evidence-based. In
October 2025 the CQC assessed the clinic and **upgraded** it from good to
outstanding.

That last sentence tends to get left out of the retellings. It belongs in.

## "Struck off" is the wrong phrase — and the difference is not pedantry

In the UK, **being struck off means erasure from the General Medical Council
register** by a fitness-to-practise tribunal. A doctor who is struck off cannot
practise medicine. It is the most serious sanction the profession has.

That is not what happened here. The British Menopause Society is a professional
membership body. Its specialist register is a **voluntary listing** that tells the
public which clinicians the BMS recognises as menopause specialists. Removing
someone from it is a meaningful professional judgement — the BMS was saying, in
public, that it no longer vouched for that practice — but it is not a licence to
practise, and losing it is not being struck off.

No GMC erasure has been reported. If you have been repeating "she was struck off"
at the school gates, this is your amnesty.

Why does this matter to you? Because the two things point in different directions.
"Struck off" invites you to conclude that everything the person ever said was
worthless. What actually happened invites a narrower and more useful question:
**was my dose in line with the guidance, or wasn't it?**

## The real story is about dose

Strip away the personalities and the row is about a specific, answerable clinical
question: how much oestrogen, and how much progestogen to go with it.

Panorama spoke to 15 women treated at the clinic. Thirteen said they had been put
on higher-than-licensed doses of HRT. Some were on three times the highest licensed
dose of oestrogen. Some had developed thickening of the womb lining — endometrial
hyperplasia — which can be a precursor to cancer. Four former Newson Health doctors
spoke to the programme with concerns of their own.

This was not the first flag. In **April 2023**, six
professional bodies — the British Menopause Society, the Faculty of Sexual and
Reproductive Healthcare, the Royal College of General Practitioners, the Royal
College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists, the Society for Endocrinology, and the
Royal College of Nursing Women's Health Forum — issued a **joint safety alert**
about high-dose oestrogen prescribing in private practice.

Its central point was straightforward: oestrogen should not routinely be prescribed
above the upper limit listed in its product licence, because those limits come from
the clinical trials that established the drug was safe. And where a higher dose is
exceptionally justified, the **progestogen must go up proportionately** — because
progestogen is what protects the womb lining from oestrogen. Under-opposed oestrogen
is precisely the mechanism behind the thickening those women experienced.

That is the whole argument, in one paragraph. It is not mystical. It is a dosing
question with a published answer.

## What the BMS actually said about testosterone

One more thing gets misquoted in both directions. In July 2024 the BMS published a
statement on testosterone in women, and it is worth reading properly, because it
is neither the anti-hormone screed nor the endorsement people claim.

The BMS said that misinformation "risks medicalising a normal life stage" and can
"render women dependent upon clinicians, some of whom may also be overly promoting
treatment with testosterone, which is associated with a high placebo response."

It also said that the only current evidence-based indication for adding testosterone
to standard HRT is **persistent low libido in postmenopausal women**, after other
contributing factors have been addressed — and that there is no good evidence that
testosterone helps other menopause symptoms, prevents bone loss, or prevents
dementia.

Note what that statement is *not*. It is not "hormones are bad." The BMS is a
strongly pro-HRT organisation. It is a warning about **treatment creep** — being
sold more intervention than the evidence supports. Those are very different claims,
and conflating them is how women end up frightened off treatment that would have
helped them.

## If you are on HRT right now, here is the useful bit

Not "stop". Please, not "stop". Stopping HRT abruptly because of a news story is a
genuinely bad plan — symptoms rebound, and for some women, particularly those who
went through menopause early, HRT is doing important long-term work.

Instead, book a review and ask three questions:

1. **What dose am I on, and how does it compare to the licensed maximum for this
   product?** You are entitled to a plain answer.
2. **If I am above the licensed dose, why — and has my progestogen been increased to
   match?** This is the safety pairing the 2023 alert was about.
3. **When was my womb lining last checked, and should it be?** Especially if you
   have had any unexpected bleeding. Unexpected bleeding on HRT always warrants a
   conversation, not a wait-and-see.

If you are not on HRT and wondering whether to be, the same principle holds in
reverse: that is a decision to make with a clinician who knows your history, not
one to make from a documentary or from a blog — including this one.

## The part that got lost in the shouting

Here is what frustrated a lot of women watching all this unfold: the argument was
conducted entirely at the level of prescriptions, as if that were the only lever
anyone had.

It isn't. Sleep, strength training, what you eat, how you handle stress, alcohol,
and how much genuine rest you get all shape how menopause feels day to day. None of
that is a replacement for medical treatment, and anyone selling it to you as one
should worry you as much as a clinic selling you triple-dose oestrogen. But it is
also not nothing, and it is the layer most women get the least support with —
because it is unglamorous, it takes weeks rather than minutes, and nobody makes a
documentary about it.

The honest framing is: **foundations underneath, medical treatment on top of them if
you and your doctor decide that is right for you.** Not either/or. Both, in the
right order.
