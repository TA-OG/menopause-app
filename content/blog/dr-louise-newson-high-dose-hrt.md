## The short version

## What actually happened

In September 2024, BBC Panorama examined prescribing at Newson Health, the private
clinic founded by Dr Louise Newson — by then more or less the face of menopause in
Britain.

They spoke to fifteen women treated there. Thirteen said they'd been put on doses
higher than the licence allows. Some were on **three times** the highest licensed
dose of oestrogen.

One of them hadn't even improved. Triple the hormones, same symptoms.

Some had developed a thickened womb lining — the phrase that should make you sit
up, because that can be a step on the road to cancer. Four doctors who used to work
at the clinic went on record with concerns of their own.

Dr Newson rejects the criticism. She says her prescribing is individualised and
evidence-based, and she has defended it publicly throughout. In October 2025 the
Care Quality Commission assessed the clinic — an inspection prompted by all of the
above — and upgraded it from good to **outstanding**.

Both things are true at once, and you should be suspicious of anyone who tells you
only one of them.

*(And on the question you probably typed into Google: she wasn't struck off. She
was dropped from the British Menopause Society's voluntary specialist register,
which is a real professional judgement but is not the medical register. She's still
practising. That's the whole distinction, and it changes nothing about your dose.)*

## The safety rule, in two sentences

Here's what the row is actually about, and it's not complicated.

Back in **April 2023** — eighteen months before any TV cameras — six professional
bodies including the Royal College of GPs and the Royal College of Obstetricians and
Gynaecologists issued a joint safety alert about high-dose oestrogen.

Their rule: oestrogen shouldn't routinely go above the upper limit on its licence,
because that limit came from the trials that showed the drug was safe. And if you
genuinely need more, **the progestogen has to go up to match.**

Why? Oestrogen thickens the womb lining. Progestogen is what keeps it in check. Too
much of one without enough of the other is precisely how those women ended up where
they did.

That's the entire scandal. A dosing question with a published answer.

## How to check your own prescription this week

You do not need to become an expert. You need one appointment and three questions.

1. **What dose am I on, and what's the licensed maximum for this product?** You are
   entitled to a straight answer. Write both numbers down.
2. **If I'm above it — why, and has my progestogen been increased to match?** This
   is the pairing the 2023 alert was about. This is the safety question.
3. **Should my womb lining be checked?** Especially if you've had any bleeding you
   weren't expecting. Unexpected bleeding on HRT is always worth a conversation,
   never a wait-and-see.

If the answers don't satisfy you, you can ask for a second opinion. That's not
being difficult. That's your prescription.

And to be very clear, because this is where women get hurt: **do not stop or reduce
anything on your own.** Symptoms rebound hard, and if you went through menopause
early your HRT is doing long-term work you can't see. Book the review.

## "But what if I don't want to be on hormones at all?"

Now the part almost nobody covered — and the reason a lot of women feel stuck.

The whole row was conducted as though prescriptions were the only lever anyone had.
So if you're now nervous about hormones, or you can't take them, it can feel like
the only option left is to grit your teeth for a decade.

That isn't true, and you don't have to take our word for it.

In **November 2024, NICE updated the national menopause guideline** to recommend
menopause-specific **cognitive behavioural therapy** for hot flushes and night
sweats — alongside HRT, or for women who can't take HRT, or for women who simply
would rather not. Not as a consolation prize. As a recommended option, in the
guideline your GP works from. The evidence behind it showed better sleep and
reduced frequency and severity of flushes, with the biggest effect on how much the
symptoms bothered women day to day.

The NHS publishes its own guidance too — including a page titled, in so many words,
*lifestyle changes to make if you're unable to take HRT*. Their list runs to
regular exercise, sleep and bedroom-temperature routines, and cutting the usual
flush triggers.

So here's the honest version of what else is on the table:

- **The trigger audit.** Caffeine, alcohol, spicy food and very hot drinks are the
  most commonly reported dietary flush triggers. The trick is removing one at a
  time for a fortnight and actually noting what happens, rather than giving
  everything up at once and learning nothing. For a lot of women the morning coffee
  turns out to be the single biggest driver.
- **Phytoestrogen foods.** Ground flaxseed, edamame, tofu, tempeh. A tablespoon or
  two of ground flaxseed stirred into porridge or yoghurt is the least dramatic
  change on this list and one of the easiest to keep up.
- **Strength and movement.** The NHS points to 150 minutes of moderate activity a
  week, and weight-bearing work matters more after menopause than before it,
  because of what happens to bone.
- **The temperature and sleep routine.** A cool bedroom, cotton sheets, lighter
  nightwear, no caffeine late. Unglamorous. Makes a real difference to night sweats.
- **CBT for menopause.** The NICE-recommended one. Available face to face, remote,
  in groups, or as self-help — though waiting lists are a genuine problem, which
  NICE itself acknowledged.

None of that is a replacement for medical treatment, and anyone selling it to you as
one should worry you as much as a clinic handing out triple doses. But it isn't
nothing either. It's the layer most women get the least help with, because it's
slow, unglamorous, and there's no headline in it.

Foundations underneath. Medical treatment on top, if you and your doctor decide
that's right. Not either/or.
