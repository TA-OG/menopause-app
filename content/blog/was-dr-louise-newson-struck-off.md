Six articles later they all contradict each other, and now you're lying there

Let's sort this out. Ten minutes, and you'll know more than the headlines told you.

## The short version

- **She wasn't struck off.** She is still a doctor, still practising.
- She was dropped from a professional register. Real, serious — but not that.
- The whole row is about **dose**. How much oestrogen, and whether enough
  progestogen came with it.
- Her clinic was inspected because of the row, and came out rated **outstanding**.
- Nothing here is a reason to stop your HRT tonight. Truly.

Now the bit that actually matters to you.

## One woman was on three times the licensed dose

In September 2024, BBC Panorama went looking at how HRT was being prescribed at
Newson Health, the private clinic founded by Dr Louise Newson — the woman who had
become, more or less, the face of menopause in Britain.

They spoke to fifteen women who'd been treated there. Thirteen said they'd been put
on doses higher than the licence allows. Some were on **three times** the highest
licensed dose of oestrogen.

One of them hadn't even got better. Triple the hormones, same symptoms.

Some had developed a thickened womb lining. That's the phrase that should make you
sit up, because a thickened womb lining can be a step on the road to cancer. Four
doctors who used to work at the clinic went on the record with concerns of their
own.

Dr Newson rejected the criticism. She says her prescribing is individualised and
evidence-based, and she has defended it publicly throughout.

Around the same time, the British Menopause Society quietly took her off its
online specialist register, saying aspects of her practice "did not accord with
established guidance."

That's the bit that became "struck off."

## It wasn't "struck off". Here's the difference

Struck off means the General Medical Council erases you from the medical register
after a fitness-to-practise tribunal. You cannot work as a doctor. It is the
heaviest thing the profession can do to you.

That is not what happened.

The British Menopause Society is a membership body. Its specialist register is a
voluntary list saying *we recognise this clinician as a menopause specialist*.
Being taken off it is a genuine, public judgement — the BMS was saying it no longer
vouched for that practice. But it isn't a licence, and losing it isn't being struck
off. No GMC erasure has been reported.

So if you've been saying "she got struck off" at the school gates — you're in good
company, and this is your amnesty.

And here's the part almost nobody repeats. In October 2025 the Care Quality
Commission went in and assessed the clinic, *because* of everything above. It came
out **upgraded** — from good to outstanding.

Both of those things are true at once. Real concerns were raised about doses. The
regulator went in and was impressed. Anyone telling you only one half of that is
selling you something.

## So what should *you* be asking?

This is the useful part. Not "was she struck off" — that changes nothing about your
morning. This changes something about your morning:

Back in April 2023, eighteen months before any of the TV cameras arrived, six
professional bodies — including the Royal College of GPs and the Royal College of
Obstetricians and Gynaecologists — put out a joint safety alert about high-dose
oestrogen.

Their point was simple. Oestrogen shouldn't routinely go above the upper limit on
its licence, because that limit came from the trials that showed the drug was safe.
And if you genuinely do need more, the **progestogen has to go up to match**.

Why? Because oestrogen thickens the womb lining and progestogen is what keeps it in
check. Too much of one without enough of the other is exactly how those women ended
up where they did.

That's the entire scandal, in two sentences. No mystery. A dosing question with a
published answer.

**So book a review and ask three things:**

1. **What dose am I actually on, and how does that compare to the licensed maximum
   for this product?** You're allowed a straight answer. Write it down.
2. **If I'm above it — why, and has my progestogen gone up to match?** This is the
   pairing the 2023 alert was about. It's the safety question.
3. **Should my womb lining be checked?** Especially if you've had any bleeding you
   weren't expecting. Unexpected bleeding on HRT is always worth a conversation,
   never a wait-and-see.

Ten minutes. One appointment. And you stop lying awake guessing.

## And while we're here: testosterone

Since it comes up in every one of these conversations.

The BMS looked at this in July 2024 and was blunt. The only evidence-based reason
to add testosterone to your HRT is **persistent low libido after menopause**, once
other causes have been ruled out. There's no good evidence it helps other symptoms,
protects your bones, or wards off dementia. And it comes, in their words, with "a
high placebo response."

They also warned that misinformation "risks medicalising a normal life stage" and
can leave women dependent on clinicians who may be over-promoting treatment.

Read that carefully, though, because it gets twisted in both directions. The BMS is
firmly pro-HRT. That statement isn't "hormones are bad." It's "don't let anyone sell
you more than the evidence supports."

Which, honestly, is a decent rule for this entire industry.

## The thing nobody made a documentary about

Here's what got lost while everyone argued about prescriptions: they were the only
lever anyone discussed.

They're not the only lever.

How you sleep. Whether you're lifting anything heavier than a kettle. What's on your
plate. How much stress you're absorbing without noticing. What alcohol is doing to
your night sweats. None of that is a replacement for medical treatment, and anyone
who tells you it is should worry you just as much as a clinic handing out triple
doses.

But it isn't nothing. It's the layer most women get the least help with — because
it's unglamorous, it takes weeks instead of minutes, and there's no headline in it.

Foundations underneath. Medical treatment on top, if you and your doctor decide
that's right. Not either/or. Both, in that order.
